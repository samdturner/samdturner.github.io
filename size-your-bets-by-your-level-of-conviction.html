Size Your Bets by Your Level of Conviction

As a founder, your objective is to find the critical path between where your product is today and product-market fit. Every week you're choosing a few things to build from a bottomless list, with a budget that runs out.

I choose with one rule. Prioritize many small, high-conviction bets over large bets based on guesses.

And prioritize the thing that makes small bets work, which is learning in the wild. Talking to users and watching real data beats theorizing in the lab, because that learning is where conviction comes from in the first place.

The rest of this post is the argument for why, and what it looks like in practice.

Problems with large bets based on guesses

1. You'll be wrong and backtrack. A large bet forces you to answer a lot of questions upfront, before the information to answer them exists. So you fill the gaps with imagination, but ideas that sound great in the office reliably fall apart in front of users. Then comes the rework of unwinding the design, rewriting the code, and re-explaining the plan. The bigger the bet, the more expensive the backtrack.

2. You'll overbuild. A large bet commits you to the full version upfront, so you never find out that a fraction of it would have been enough. You spend a month polishing something that half a day would have satisfied, and every hour past "enough" is budget stolen from the next most important thing.

3. You won't build customer understanding. With a large bet, all the learning arrives at the end, after the budget is spent. You can work for months and finish knowing no more about your customer than when you started. That's the quietest cost of the three so far, and maybe the biggest. You paid full price and didn't even buy information.

4. You'll waste time debating. When nobody has real information, decisions come down to who's most persuasive, and persuasiveness has nothing to do with being right. Big speculative bets generate exactly these debates, because there's so much surface area to disagree about and no evidence to settle any of it. The arguments burn time, and worse, they burn morale.

An example: building feedback in five small bets

When we set out to build the feedback feature for our job interview prep product, we had exactly one piece of high conviction. Users needed feedback on their interview answers. It was the overwhelming request coming out of our user interviews. However, the details of what ideal feedback looked like were a guess. We started with a long list of unknowns:

  • Should the feedback be written or verbal?
  • Should we show a score, and if so, a number, a letter grade, an emoji, or something else?
  • Should we include metrics? If so, what metrics?
  • How do we know if the feedback is helpful?
  • How do we know if it's accurate?
  • Will users actually trust it?
  • Consolidated feedback for the whole interview, or separate feedback for every answer?
  • Include the transcript of what they said?
  • Track progress across sessions?
  • How much text would anyone actually read?
  • Allow users to ask clarifying questions?
  • ....and more.

It would have been tempting to design the perfect version upfront. But a big bet placed on that many guesses would have meant burning budget and backtracking. So we sized our bet to our conviction. We shipped the most basic version of feedback we could. It was far from ideal (almost embarassingly so), but that was fine. It gave users something real to react to, and each reaction informed the next bet.

Six and a half days. That's the entire budget this feature consumed, from first design to final QA, spread across five weekly sprints. And it was never our sole focus. Each of those sprints also carried bets in other areas of the business, so this feature came to life in the margins of a normal roadmap.

And how do we know the feedback actually works, and that we're done? Our users told us.

“I like how in depth it goes. I agree with the feedback, and it's very useful.”

“I like that it gives you a rating on different areas so you can see where you're strong.”

“I really like the visual cues. I think it's really great feedback.”

“I like this better than ChatGPT. Chat always agrees with you.”

We asked these same questions about Bet 1, and the feedback was entirely negative. Five bets later, the negatives had dried up to a few minor nice-to-haves, even when we pressed for criticism. That's the signal to stop. The next bet belonged somewhere else in the product.

There's one more way to look at those five bets. Each one was a step toward clarity, answering questions from our original list and driving down the number of unknowns until the guesses were gone and only conviction remained.

Benefits of small bets

1. Backtracking disappears. Every bet is placed on conviction you've already earned, so there's minimul throwaway work. There's a simple way to see why. Put every feature decision on a 2x2. Did users need it, and did you build it?

Three of these quadrants are fine. Even the one that surprises people, they needed it and you haven't built it yet, is fine. Shipping something a few weeks later is not going to change the trajectory of your startup. You can afford to wait a few sprints until you've earned the conviction to place the bet.

The only fatal quadrant is you built it and they didn't need it, and it's fatal in proportion to what you spent. Half a day? That's a cheap price for the learning. Three months? Enough of these failures and it will kill the business.

2. Overbuilding disappears too. Sometimes you ship the most basic version and discover it's enough. The user is satisfied, the box is checked, and you can stop and redirect that budget to something more important. The basic version isn't just a test of what to build next. It's a test of whether you need to build anything more at all, and "no" is one of the most valuable answers you can get.

3. Customer understanding compounds. Every bet returns two things, the feature and the learning. And those learnings directly inform your future bets, where to place them, how to size them, and which ones to skip entirely. Five sprints into our feedback feature, we didn't just have a better product. We had a better mental model of our customer.

4. The need for debate fades. This happens for two reasons. First, you're constantly gathering evidence, and it's much easier to agree on evidence than on opinions. With enough evidence, the answers usually become obvious.

Second, the team doesn't need to get it perfect in any single sprint. Real feedback is a week away, so if you're wrong in sprint one, you'll fix it in sprint two. That takes the pressure off, and there's no reason to spend hours debating the perfect version.

What it takes to run this way

There's one hard requirement. Weekly sprints with real user contact. Small bets only work if the learning loop is fast. If you ship a small bet and then wait a month for feedback, you get all the slowness of small steps with none of the payoff.

The point, one more time

You have a bottomless list of things you could build and a finite budget. Spend it in small pieces, sized to your conviction, and let your users tell you what deserves more.

A large bet is just a guess with a plan attached. The fastest way to build the right thing is to never bet big on a guess.
