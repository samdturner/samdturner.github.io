Make High Quality Decisions

Moving fast matters. It's how you find product market fit before you run out of runway. Most choices are reversible, so the right move is to decide fast, adjust, and keep moving. I've written about how we make those quick calls in Not Every Decision Deserves a Meeting.

But a small number of decisions are important and hard to reverse. Choose your core technology wrong, and you don't adjust, you rebuild and lose months. For these decisions, you need to balance speed and care. As Robert Martin put it, "the only way to go fast, is to go well."

This post covers the framework we use at Lira for important, irreversible decisions. Then I'll walk through a real example, how we chose our real-time voice technology.

Two things decide how much process a decision deserves: how much it matters, and whether you can walk it back.

The principles

  1. Set the context. Before anything else, write down what's happening in the business. Why is this decision on the table now? What constraints exist? A decision only makes sense inside its context.
  2. Define your goal. Know what a good outcome looks like before you compare anything. If you can't state the goal, you're not ready to evaluate options.
  3. Decide who decides. Assign a single owner. Decisions without an owner drift, get relitigated, or never happen at all.
  4. Lay out the options. List every realistic option, including the boring ones.
  5. For each option, write the pros, cons, risks, and unknowns. Writing it down forces clarity. The gaps in your knowledge become visible, and those gaps are usually where bad decisions hide.
  6. Drive down the unknowns until you have conviction. Run spikes. Talk to vendors. Test your assumptions. Keep going until you're confident about the best option.
  7. Decide. Once you have conviction, commit and move.

Where this comes from

This process is adapted from SPADE, a decision-making framework used at Square. SPADE stands for Setting, People, Alternatives, Decide, Explain. We used it often there, and it stuck with me. What we run at Lira is a lighter-weight version, adapted for a two person team where the decider and the builders are the same people.

Example: choosing our real-time voice technology

The context

This was April 2025. Real-time voice technology was very new, and the landscape was shifting month to month. New options were launching constantly and pricing kept changing.

At the same time, we had just deployed Instruct, our training product, to its first customer. We were getting signal that a sales training product might perform well, and we were exploring a pivot from a general purpose training product to a sales specific one. Realistic voice role play would be the core of that product. So the voice technology choice mattered a lot.

The goal

We needed a voice experience real enough that a sales rep would practice against it and forget, at least a little, that they were talking to an AI.

Who decided

I owned the decision. My co-founder Wenyu was involved in reviewing it, but one person owned driving it to a conclusion.

The requirements

This was tricky because the requirements pulled in three directions at once.

Three of these deserve a note. Scope is really a question about how much of the stack you own: an all-in-one agent platform hands you a lot, a stitched-together pipeline hands you control and a build list. Cost at scale matters because a price that works for a demo can quietly kill you at volume. And the user column is the one specs can't answer, which is why most of the week went there.

Step 1Research nine options

We started wide. The list was GPT-4o, GPT-4o mini, ElevenLabs Conversational AI, Deepgram Voice Agent, Deepgram plus ElevenLabs, Gemini 2.5 Flash, LiveKit, Vapi, and Retell.

For each one, we mapped the same criteria on a board. Time to implement. Product quality, covering voice realism, voice emotion, response intelligence, and latency. Scalability, covering rate limits, concurrency limits, and pricing scalability. Cost per hour of interactivity. And unknowns, the questions we couldn't answer yet.

Some options eliminated themselves. Gemini 2.5 Flash couldn't generate audio output at the time. Retell's cost was too high for our margins. Others fell out on latency or concurrency risks. We ended with a short list of four.

Step 2Drive down the unknowns with blind tests

The board answered the technical and business questions. It couldn't answer the user question: which one actually feels real. Specs don't tell you that. So we tested.

I set up mock sales role plays with each of the four finalists and ran them with my co-founder, without telling him which option was which. Blind testing mattered because we already had opinions about these vendors, and we wanted to judge the experience, not the brand.

We ran three rounds. After each round, we wrote down what worked well and what needed to improve, and we ranked every pain point on a severity scale from one, an observation, up to five, a deal breaker. Then we improved each prototype before the next round.

The details that decided realness were smaller than we expected. Whether it sometimes gave short answers instead of paragraphs. Whether its emotional tone matched what it was saying. Whether it repeated the same phrases. One version lost points just for sighing too often. After three rounds, a clear winner had emerged.

Step 3Validate with real users

We took the top two options into user interviews. We wanted to know how real each one felt to people in the wild, not just to the two of us who had been staring at them for weeks. Their feedback confirmed what the blind tests showed.

The decision, and something better than a decision

We chose Vapi. We had conviction because it held up at every stage: on the evaluation board, in the blind tests, and with real users, across the technical, business, and user requirements.

But we walked away with more than a decision. Because we prototyped and improved through every round, the winner wasn't a name on a whiteboard. It was working software. Latency was measured. Costs were understood. Realness was validated by real users. All the important requirements were already derisked, so translating the prototype into a product was a short step, not a fresh project.

The whole thing took six days

This might sound like a heavy process. It wasn't. From first research to final decision, it took six days.

Rigor and speed aren't opposites. The structure is what made it fast, because every day had a clear job.

The product died, the decision lived

Here's the twist. We ended up not pursuing the sales training product, for reasons unrelated to the voice technology. But the decision still paid off. We use Vapi in Lira today, our job interview prep product. The choice survived a pivot because we made it on fundamentals, not on a single use case.

Takeaways

  • Funnel wide, then narrow fast. Nine options became four, became two, became one.
  • Unknowns are where bad decisions hide. Spend your spike time there.
  • Blind tests remove bias you don't know you have.
  • Real users are the final judge of user requirements.
  • A good process can hand you more than a decision. If you drive down unknowns by building, you end with a derisked prototype instead of a document.

Then decide with conviction, and measure the results.
