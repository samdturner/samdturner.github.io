Make High Quality Decisions

Moving fast matters. It's how you find product market fit before you run out of resources. Most choices are reversible, so the right move is to decide fast, adjust, and keep moving. I've written about how we make those quick calls in Not Every Decision Deserves a Meeting.

But a small number of decisions are important and hard to reverse. Choose your core technology wrong, and you don't adjust, you rebuild and lose months. For these decisions, you need to balance speed and care. As Robert Martin put it, "the only way to go fast, is to go well."

This post covers the framework we use at Lira for important, irreversible decisions. Then I'll walk through a real example, how we chose our real-time voice technology.

Two things decide how much process a decision deserves: how much it matters, and whether you can walk it back.

The framework

Our framework is adapted from SPADE, a decision-making framework we used often at Square. Ours is a lighter-weight version, meant for a lean team with minimal process.

  1. Set context. Write down what's happening in the business, why this decision is on the table now, and what constraints exist.
  2. Define the goal. Know what a good outcome looks like before you compare anything.
  3. Assign ownership. Give the decision an owner. Without one, decisions can drag on for ages.
  4. List options. Include every realistic option, even the boring ones.
  5. Evaluate the options. Write each option's pros, cons, risks, and unknowns. The unknowns are where the gotchas hide.
  6. Drive down unknowns. Conduct research, run spikes, talk to users, and test assumptions until you have conviction.
  7. Decide. Commit and move on.

Example: selecting our real-time voice technology

The context

This was April 2025. Real-time voice technology was very new, and the landscape was shifting month to month. New options were launching constantly and pricing kept changing.

At the same time, we had just deployed Instruct, our training product, to its first customer. We were getting signal that a sales training product might perform well, and we were exploring a pivot from a general purpose training product to a sales specific one. Realistic voice role play would be the core of that product. So the voice technology choice mattered a lot.

The goal

We needed a voice experience real enough that a sales rep would practice against it and forget, at least a little, that they were talking to an AI.

Who decided

I owned the decision. My co-founder was involved in reviewing it, but one person owned driving it to a conclusion.

The requirements

There was a long list of requirements a real-time voice solution had to meet, and they spanned three different categories. Some could be answered by reading the documentation. Others could only be answered by prototyping with real users.

  • Technical. Latency, concurrency limits, and scope.
  • Business. Cost, cost at scale, and cash flow. A price that works for a demo can quietly kill you at volume.
  • User. Realness, which breaks into voice realism and response intelligence.

Three of those requirements were hard limits. An option that missed one was out, however good it looked everywhere else.

  • $7 or lessper hour

    Cost per hour of interactivity is what we pay every time a rep practices. Past $7 there isn't enough room left between what a session costs us and what a customer will pay for it.

  • 10 or moreconcurrent sessions

    Enough to run user tests without a queue. We assumed that whatever we picked, real scale would be a conversation with the vendor later, so we only needed the early tests to work.

  • 5 days or lessto build

    We're a lean team and time to market is what validates the product. Days spent assembling basic voice infrastructure are days not spent finding out whether anyone wants it.

Step 1Research nine options

We started wide. The list was GPT-4o, GPT-4o mini, ElevenLabs Conversational AI, Deepgram Voice Agent, Deepgram plus ElevenLabs, Gemini 2.5 Flash, LiveKit, Vapi, and Retell, plus the option of building our own.

For each one, we mapped the same criteria on a board. Time to implement. Product quality, covering voice realism, response intelligence, and latency. Scalability, covering rate limits, concurrency limits, and pricing scalability. Cost per hour of interactivity. And unknowns, the questions we couldn't answer yet.

Here is how they stacked up on the technical and business requirements.

Some options eliminated themselves. Gemini 2.5 Flash couldn't generate audio output at the time. Retell's cost was too high for our margins. Others fell out on latency or concurrency risks. We ended with a short list of four.

Step 2Test the options against the user requirements

The research answered the technical and business questions. It couldn't tell us which option actually felt real. So we tested.

I set up mock sales role plays with each of the four finalists and ran them with my co-founder. He didn't know which option was which. Blind testing mattered because we already had opinions about these vendors. We wanted to judge the experience without being biased by the brand.

We ran three test rounds. After each round we scored every prototype out of five on the two user requirements, voice realism and response intelligence. Then we improved each prototype before the next round.

By the end of the third round we had worked out the kinks, and the best version felt close to real. We had a clear winner. Vapi scored highest on voice realism and emotion, was the fastest to implement, and its pay as you go pricing fit our cash flow. Gemini 2.5 Flash made the responses smart at a cost that worked at scale.

The pick came with trade-offs. Vapi wasn't the cheapest, GPT-4o mini cost about a third as much. And raising Vapi's default limit of ten concurrent calls would cost extra. But both were fine at our size, and neither was a one way door. The bigger caveat was that only the two of us had judged it. So we treated Vapi as the pick, pending real users.

Step 3Test with real users

We had a winner, but only two people had judged it, and we built it. The last unknown was whether it would feel real to strangers.

We recruited four people from the target market, two sales reps and two sales managers. Each one shared their screen and ran a sales role play against our prototype. We still hadn't written any production code. The whole thing ran in Vapi using Gemini 2.5 Flash.

After each session we ran a short debrief. We asked how real the interaction felt and how ready it was to train real people, both on a scale of one to five. Then they rated each part of the experience, voice quality, emotional realism, latency, challenge, and content, from dealbreaker to impressive.

The sessions confirmed the blind tests. Ratings landed at mostly realistic. Latency impressed people. And they told us where the gaps were, like wanting to be able to change to difficulty level.

The whole thing took five days

This might sound like a heavy process. It wasn't. From first research to final decision, it took about five days. What made it fast was focus. Every day we drove down unknowns, getting clearer on how each option stacked up against the requirements. By Friday we had enough information to make a confident decision.

The product died, the decision lived

Here's the twist. We never built the sales training product. We killed it soon after, for reasons that had nothing to do with voice technology.

But the decision outlived the product. We use Vapi in Lira today, our job interview prep product. The choice survived a pivot because we made it on fundamentals, real requirements, tested options, and derisked unknowns, not on a single use case. Five days of care bought us a choice we haven't had to revisit since.
