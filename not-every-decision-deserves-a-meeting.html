Reduce Meeting Overhead: Not Every Decision Deserves a Meeting

An ambiguous decision just landed on you. You're already drafting the Slack message, looping in four people, asking when everyone's free to talk it through.

But before you hit send, you stop yourself. "Is a meeting really worth it?" Four people for an hour is half a day of build time, plus the context switch, plus the delay in getting to an answer.

Maybe you should just decide and go. Maybe the decision can wait. Maybe it does deserve the meeting.

These three factors can help you decide: urgency, impact, and reversibility.

Factor 1Urgency: decide at the last responsible moment

Not every decision needs to be made right this second. A simple test: will this change what anyone does imminently? If not, it can wait.

Waiting has two benefits. First, you get more information. Decisions often feel hard because you're missing the information to get to clarity. Wait until it exists, and the right choice becomes clear. Second, sometimes the need to decide goes away. Priorities shift, a constraint disappears, the question answers itself.

Agile development calls this making decisions at the last responsible moment. The key word is responsible. It's the point just before waiting starts to cost you. Past that you're procrastinating.

Factor 2Impact: most decisions don't move the needle

A handful of decisions will determine the fate of the business. Many don't move the needle at all. The trick is recognizing which one is in front of you before you've spent an afternoon on it.

High impact

  • Who are we building for?
  • What problem does our product solve?
  • How are we better than the competition?

Low impact

  • Button padding and spacing
  • Which date library to use
  • Whether the empty state says "No results" or "Nothing here yet"

The failure mode is letting the low-impact decisions consume your resources, and then running out of time before you figure out what to build, who to build it for, and how to reach them.

Still, it's tempting to focus on low-impact decisions. They're concrete and approachable. Everyone can picture the empty state, so everyone has something to contribute. But when every opinion is equally reasonable, the decision doesn't need a debate. It needs an owner.

Factor 3Reversibility: can you walk it back?

Jeff Bezos popularized a powerful mental model for this. Think of decisions as either one-way doors or two-way doors. Two-way doors are reversible. If the choice doesn't work out, you walk it back at little cost, so make these fast. Ship, learn, adjust. One-way doors are costly to undo, and everything built afterwards inherits them, so they deserve care.

There's one more door worth naming. Your time is the ultimate one-way door. Once you've spent it on something, you never get it back. That's true whether you spend it building the wrong thing or debating a decision that didn't need the meeting.

Putting it together

When a decision lands on you, three questions in order.

  1. Does this need deciding now? If not, defer the decision.
  2. What breaks if I get it wrong? If you can't name anything, it's low impact. Assign an owner and let them decide.
  3. Can I undo it? If yes, decide fast and watch closely. If no, decide carefully, together.

Most decisions leave through one of the first three exits. The rare one that reaches the far end has earned the room. It has to be decided now, it shapes what comes after, and you can't walk it back. Book the meeting.

The builders who ship fast aren't always the ones better at deciding. They're better at noticing which decisions deserve the time. Many decisions don't.
