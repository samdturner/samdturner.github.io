Rise of the Product Engineer: How AI-Native Teams Ship Absurdly Fast

A product engineer is one person who does three jobs. They figure out what users need, design the solution, and build it. Product management, design, and engineering collapsed into a single role.

Teams built this way ship dramatically faster than traditional ones. A handful of companies are organizing around product engineers, including my startup Lira, and the difference in speed is not subtle.

Almost everyone still works the old way. You have a product manager, a few engineers, and a designer if the work touches users. The PM decides what to build, the designer makes it specific, an engineer builds it. Each person owns their station and passes the work along.

Every handoff between those stations costs something. Context gets compiled, transferred, and lost. Work sits in queues. Decisions get made with half the picture. Nobody even thinks of these as costs. They've been there so long they just look like work. But they were always the price of specialization, paid because there was no way around it. No single person could do quality work across all three jobs.

That just changed.

Why now

AI is opening the door to the product engineer role by elevating each team member's adjacent skills. An engineer with mediocre design taste is now a pretty good designer. A designer who could sort of code now writes real code. Someone who has never run a user interview can prep one, structure it, and pull the themes out of the transcript.

The skill gap is closing. The cost of splitting the work is not.

Here's what it costs.

The five taxes of specialized roles

1. Handoff cost

Every boundary between two people is a context transfer, and both ends pay. The sender writes a PRD or spec, which takes days and none of which ships. The receiver reads it, sits through a walkthrough, and absorbs it before any real work starts.

2. Queue time

Work spends most of its life waiting. A design sits three days waiting on review, a ticket sits in the backlog until next sprint, a question sits in Slack until someone gets out of meetings. Compare a feature's calendar time against the hours anyone actually spent on it, and the gap is almost all queue. And it's invisible, because everyone's calendar is full and nobody owns the waiting.

3. Back and forth

No document survives contact with the work. The questions that matter only come up once the receiver is deep in the details, and each one starts a round trip: ask, wait for the PM or designer to be free, get an answer, discover the answer raises another question. Each round costs hours or days, and while it's open, the work is blocked.

4. Idle capacity

The throughput of a system is limited by its slowest resource. So unless your system is kept perfectly balanced, you'll pay a tax in the form of idle capacity. In practice, this means some team members will feel underutilized and others will feel overwhelmed.

5. Split-context decisions

The taxes above cost time. This one costs decision quality.

Options come from context. Product knows the user and the business case. Engineering knows what things cost to build. Split those across two heads, and each side can only propose options from their half of the picture.

The problem is that the best option often sits in the overlap, cheap to build and good for users, and neither side can see it. The team debates option A against option B when the right answer was option C, which nobody ever said out loud.

Enter the product engineer

Product engineers minimize these taxes. Collapse the roles into one and the boundaries those taxes live on simply aren't there anymore.

No handoffs. Work goes from user problem to design to implementation without changing hands. There's no context to transfer because it never leaves the person who has it.

No queue. The next step starts when the last one finishes, because the same person does both. Work stays out of the backlog.

No back and forth. The questions that come up during implementation can be answered by the person they occur to. Work doesn't block waiting on a clarification.

No idle capacity. Every product engineer is a complete unit, so the system is balanced by construction. Add one and you ship faster. Nobody sits idle waiting for another role to catch up.

No split context. Every decision gets made with the full picture in one head. Technical risks get flagged when they're cheapest to route around, and the spec can change mid-build, because the person who built it owns the reasoning behind it. Best of all, the option C gets found: the person weighing what users need is the same person who knows what's cheap to build.

What this looks like in practice

Here's a real example, start to finish.

Lira builds interview prep guides for job seekers, and they're only useful if they're about the actual job you're interviewing for. Originally we took a job title and a company name and searched for the posting in the background. It mostly worked.

1. Problem discovered in user interviews. Some people didn't trust it. They had no way to know we'd found the right posting, and a guide you don't trust is a guide you don't use.

2. Started with the obvious solution. Let people paste a job URL. Users know exactly which posting they mean, we fetch it, trust problem solved.

3. Technical spike revealed a gotcha. We learned that job boards block scrapers. LinkedIn and Indeed simply don't let you fetch a posting from a URL.

4. Second spike looked at third-party job data vendors. I evaluated three, and all three shared the same problems. Postings refreshed too slowly and went stale. Pricing that impacted our bottom line. And no vendor can fix the failure I kept seeing in testing, where users paste the wrong URL.

5. Realized the URL is not the key. An engineer handed a spec is thinking that their job is to fetch a job posting from a URL. The spike results mean the job is blocked, so they escalate and wait. I was working from a different definition of the job, which was to help users get a prep guide for their actual job. The URL was never the job. It was one candidate mechanism, and the spikes had just eliminated it.

6. Redesigned around the real job. Skip the URL entirely. Let people paste the job description text itself. No scraper, no vendor, no wrong URL problem, and it took under half a day to build.

7. Users voted with their actions. We shipped it alongside the original company and title option, and about 70% of people chose to paste the description.

Every step of that loop happened inside one mind, and shipped in less than one day.

Objection #1: specialization beats generalization

Jack of all trades, master of none. This is the argument most people will reach for, and it's a fair one. A dedicated designer produces better designs than an engineer. A dedicated PM does deeper research.

But specialists were never free. You pay for the depth with all the taxes the last few sections described. For years that price was worth paying, because the gap between a specialist and a generalist was enormous. AI shrank the gap. The coordination costs stayed exactly the same. So the trade that made sense for decades quietly stopped making sense. You're no longer giving up a great design to get a mediocre one. You're giving up a great design to get a good one, plus everything that comes with speed.

And there's a deeper problem with paying for depth. Depth is only worth a lot when you already know what to build. Early on, you usually don't. A perfectly built version of the wrong idea loses to three quick attempts at finding the right one.

Objection #2: you removed the check

In the traditional model, the friction between product and engineering is also a check. The PM stops the engineer from quietly cutting the thing users needed. The engineer stops the PM from committing to six weeks of work. Collapse the roles and nobody is there to say no. The product engineer is grading their own homework.

The check doesn't disappear. It moves outside the team. We run a fixed sprint cadence where something goes in front of real users every cycle, no exceptions, and the results get measured. A product engineer has a lot of freedom inside a sprint, but they are held accountable for the results.

That's what the 70% was in the last story. Nobody approved the option to paste the job post. Users approved it by using it.

And that's a better check. An internal debate gets settled by who is more persuasive. Users settle it with behavior. The old check told you what the team believed. The new one tells you whether you were right.

Objection #3: these people are hard to find

I agree with this one, and it's why adoption of this model will be slow.

I've worked with a few hundred engineers, and I can't think of too many who would obviously enjoy this role. Most people became engineers because they like technical problems. They didn't sign up to sit with users and design interfaces. And even among those who want it, doing all three jobs well is a different question. Great engineers aren't usually great designers, and AI tools won't always close that gap.

The competitive edge

Most product teams are still set up the traditional way, and I predict most will stay that way for the foreseeable future. So the companies that do find these people, and hand them real ownership, will ship meaningfully faster than their competitors. To the outside world, and to the competitors, the product will improve faster than anyone can explain.

It's not because anyone is working harder. They just stopped paying the avoidable taxes of coordination.
