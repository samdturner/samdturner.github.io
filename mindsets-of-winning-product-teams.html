Mindsets of Winning Product Teams

Mindsets shape the hundreds of decisions behind every product, from which feature to build next, to when a release is good enough to ship, to which user complaint to act on, to which metric to trust, to when to move on from an idea.

Winning products come from winning mindsets. Here are the ones I've found work, along with the anti-patterns to avoid.

Winning mindsets

Own the user problem, not just code

Ownership does not just span product spec to passing tests. It starts with defining the user problem, then designing the solution, implementing it, measuring the impact, and iterating on it. The person who works this way is called a product engineer.

When one person can carry an idea from user interview to shipped feature, there are no handoffs between product, design, and engineering, no meetings to align three functions, and no context lost in translation. The loop from insight to iteration runs in days instead of quarters.

Obsess over the customer

Writing code is cheap now. An AI coding agent can write it, make the tests pass, and make sure it compiles. Understanding the customer is the part that cannot be delegated. Companies rarely fail because the code was imperfect. They fail because they solved a problem people do not care enough to pay for, or never reached the people who do.

So the obsession belongs with the customer. Know who they are, what they struggle with, what words they use to describe it, and where they go looking for solutions. That knowledge compounds, and it is one of the hardest advantages to copy.

Fit the product to the business model

A great product is not enough. The product is one piece of a larger business model, and every piece has to fit together, from the customer segment and pricing to distribution and unit economics. A product that delights users but cannot reach them, or cannot be priced profitably, still fails as a business.

So build the product to fit the model. When the pieces fit, they form one cohesive customer factory, a machine that finds customers, serves them well, and earns money doing it.

Think of a product as a pile of risks

A new product is an onion made of risks, and it succeeds once they have all been peeled away. Investors call this the onion theory of risk. Can the team build it? Do people need it, and will they pay? Is the market big enough? Can customers be acquired profitably, at scale?

Start with the biggest risk you can realistically test. If the test fails, you have failed quickly, with time left to change course. The winners are not the teams that avoid risk. They are the teams that retire it fastest.

Make small, evidence-backed bets

Most founders know the feeling of falling in love with an idea at the whiteboard, launching it, and discovering nobody cares. The only judge that matters is the user. So get the product into their hands as early as possible, and leave yourself time to course correct from something they ignore to something they cannot live without. Talk to users constantly, ship into the wild, and measure what happens. Each loop is a small bet, and the evidence it returns tells you where to place the next one.

(More on how to size those bets in Size Your Bets by Your Level of Conviction.)

Embrace forcing functions

Product development is messy and can run forever, so it needs a time constraint. A forcing function is a commitment that makes the work converge into some improvement the customers can experience. "We ship on Friday, and then we measure whether we actually solved the problem." "We run user interviews on Monday to see how people react to the new feature."

A time constraint works because it keeps the team focused on the set of features that matter, and forces them to cut what doesn't.

Use plain-spoken language

Plain-spoken language means describing the work in words humans can understand.

Not this

"We're an end-to-end financial operations platform empowering SMBs to unlock cash-flow efficiencies."

Say this

"We make it easy for freelancers to send invoices and get paid."

Complicated language makes the listener work to decode it, which slows the team down and puts people off. Simple words keep everyone in the conversation and help the team solve problems faster.

Bring positive energy

The best conversations leave you energized, amped up, motivated to go solve the next problem. The worst leave you drained. Everyone knows which kind of person they would rather work with. Be that person.

Keep refining your craft

Product development is a craft, and part of the joy comes from improving, finding ways to achieve more with less. A few habits make that improvement steady. Give feedback generously and receive it gracefully. Hold team retros to talk about what went well and what could have gone better. Week after week, the improvements add up.

Anti-patterns

Just as important as the mindsets are the anti-patterns, the habits that quietly slow a startup down.

Solutions-first thinking

Solutions-first thinking is falling in love with a solution and then hunting for a problem to justify it, like a person with a hammer looking for nails. A technology in search of a problem usually finds a market of nobody.

Work in the other direction. Start with the problem and work backwards to the solution.

Bad

"AI agents are a cool new technology. Let's figure out how to use them to improve the lives of construction workers."

Good

"Construction supervisors lose hours every week to compliance paperwork. What is the best way to make it disappear? An AI agent might be the answer."

Chasing Silicon Valley hype

Silicon Valley hype is always with us, from social media apps to blockchain to NFTs to the metaverse to whatever comes next. Chasing it is a poor substitute for thinking. Reason from first principles and build a real business that creates real value for users.

Benjamin Graham said it best. "You are neither right nor wrong because the crowd disagrees with you." What makes you right is that your facts and your reasoning are sound. The same holds for products. A technology being hot says nothing about whether it solves your users' problem.

Overplanning

Plans change the moment real users touch the product. Shipping something small and putting it in their hands will teach you more than months invested in the perfect planning document with everyone's input. Plan just enough to ship the next thing, and let what you learn shape the rest.

Lengthy discussions and approval chains

Long discussions and approval chains slow teams down, and nothing drains a builder like waiting for permission. Worse, debates on Zoom do not produce learning. Shipped products and real data do.

So maximize the time spent shipping product and learning from real users. Most debates can be settled faster by shipping something small and measuring what happens. Decide, ship, measure, repeat.

Perfecting the code

This is the hardest one for engineers to unlearn, myself included after years of following clean coding practices. Every hour spent polishing the code has an opportunity cost. It is time not spent finding product-market fit. The end goal is not better code. The goal is a better business. The code is a means, not the end.

Bad

"We can't ship yet. I want to rewrite the backend in Rust first."

Good

"This is solid enough to put in front of users this week. If the feature takes off, we'll harden it."

Blindly copying others

Copying what others are doing, without understanding why it worked for them, is a trap. What works in one context will not work in another. Pick the best tool for the job by reasoning up from first principles. Define the goals, lay out the alternatives, and weigh the pros / cons / risks / unknowns of each.

Bad

"We should switch to Cassandra. That's what Netflix uses."

Good

"We chose Postgres because our data is relational, the team knows it well, and one database keeps operations simple."

Unnecessary technical complexity

Complexity slows a team down. It's more expensive to build up front and more expensive to change, and it increases the risk of running out of resources before hitting product-market fit. Strive instead for the simplest design that solves the user's problem and meets the business requirements. Add complexity only when it is justified.

Bad

"We have a hundred users, so we split the backend into twelve microservices on Kubernetes, with an event bus, a service mesh, and multi-region failover, so we're ready when we hit millions of users."

Good

"We have a hundred users, so we run one web server, a database, and a background worker, all hosted and managed by Render."

Justified complexity

"We have millions of users and growing, so we added caching, queues, and multi-region infrastructure because the load demands it."
